In South Australia, a rental property must meet the minimum housing standards before the tenant moves in. The standards are set by the [Housing Improvement Regulations 2017](https://www.legislation.sa.gov.au/_legislation-documents/lz/c/r/housing-improvement-regulations-2017/current/2017.17.auth.pdf), made under the Housing Improvement Act 2016, and since 1 July 2024 the [Residential Tenancies Act 1995](https://www.legislation.sa.gov.au/_legislation-documents/lz/c/a/residential-tenancies-act-1995/current/1995.63.auth.pdf) makes meeting them at the start of a tenancy the landlord's job.

Below is what that means in practice, as at October 2026, and a checklist you can work through before you let.

## Who sets the standards

The minimum housing standards are prescribed in the Housing Improvement Regulations 2017. The [Housing Safety Authority](https://housingsafetyauthority.sa.gov.au/minimum-housing-standards), part of the SA Government, administers them and publishes room-by-room guidance and a [minimum housing standards checklist (HIA-07)](https://www.housingsafetyauthority.sa.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/1084741/Minimum-Housing-Standards-Checklist-HIA-07.pdf).

Consumer and Business Services (CBS) looks after the renting side: the tenancy laws that say when the standards have to be met and what a tenant can do if they aren't. Its [modifications and minimum standards](https://cbs.sa.gov.au/sections/renting/renting/modifications-and-minimum-standards) page is the plain-English summary.

## What changed in July 2024

South Australia's [rental reforms](https://www.cbs.sa.gov.au/campaigns/rental-reforms) took effect on 1 July 2024. According to the [Housing Safety Authority](https://housingsafetyauthority.sa.gov.au/private-rental-reforms), the changes that affect the minimum housing standards are:

- Landlords and agents must make sure the property meets the minimum housing standards at the beginning of a tenancy (section 67A of the Residential Tenancies Act).
- A tenant can ask the landlord or agent to carry out urgent repairs to bring the property up to the standards.
- A tenant can serve a notice of termination if the property doesn't meet the standards.
- The grounds for not renewing a fixed-term lease are restricted.

Not meeting the standards at the start of a tenancy is an offence. [CHECK: search results from legislation.sa.gov.au and the CBS penalties table show a maximum penalty of $25,000 and a $1,200 expiation fee for section 67A(1). Confirm against the current Act before mentioning a figure.]

Further minor changes to the tenancy laws started on 15 January 2026. CBS's [summary of tenancy reforms for landlords and agents](https://cbs.sa.gov.au/documents/tenancy/fact-sheets/Summary-of-tenancy-reforms-landlords-and-agents.pdf) covers them. ## Pre-letting checklist

This follows the Housing Safety Authority's guidance on the standards. Use the official [HIA-07 checklist](https://www.housingsafetyauthority.sa.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/1084741/Minimum-Housing-Standards-Checklist-HIA-07.pdf) as the final word, and get a qualified tradesperson to look at anything you're unsure about. [CHECK: the official pages blocked direct access while this was written, so the items below come from Housing Safety Authority search results. Check each one against HIA-07.]

### The whole property

- Structurally sound, weatherproof and effectively drained (no roof leaks or broken guttering)
- Reasonably free from mould and damp
- No build-up of rubbish, no fire hazards and no vermin
- A continuous electricity supply and enough power points
- Fixtures and fittings properly installed, fit for purpose and working

### Smoke alarms

- Smoke alarms fitted and working, placed to suit the building's classification under the National Construction Code

### Doors, windows and lighting

- Every external door has a lock that works with a key from outside and opens from inside without a key ([external doors, windows and lighting](https://www.housingsafetyauthority.sa.gov.au/minimum-housing-standards/external-doors,-windows-and-lighting))
- A way for the tenant to screen visitors safely, such as a window, lockable screen door, peephole, security chain or intercom
- Every external window that opens has a working latch
- At least one electric light in each room, and adequate ventilation in living areas, the bathroom, toilet and laundry

### Kitchen

- A working sink with hot and cold water, and drinking-quality water from the sink
- A working oven and cooktop
- Enough bench space to prepare food, and a cupboard or pantry for storing it

### Bathroom, toilet and laundry

- A toilet, a bath or shower, and a hand basin, with privacy, hot and cold water and proper drainage
- A wash trough or basin, plus water and drainage for a washing machine

### Clean and in repair

The Residential Tenancies Act also says the property must be in a reasonable state of cleanliness when the tenant moves in (section 67). It must also be in a reasonable state of repair, judged against its age, character and likely lifespan, and kept that way during the tenancy (section 68).

For cleaning, presentation and photos, see [how to get your property ready to rent](/blog/how-to-prepare-your-property-for-rent/).

## Smoke alarms: the landlord's responsibility

The [Metropolitan Fire Service](https://mfs.sa.gov.au/community-safety/safety-and-education/fact-sheets/smoke-alarms) says that in a rental home, the owner is responsible for installing and maintaining smoke alarms. The type of alarm you need depends on when you bought the property:

- **Bought before 1 February 1998:** a replaceable battery smoke alarm, at minimum.
- **Bought on or after 1 February 1998:** a 240-volt mains-powered alarm, or a 10-year non-replaceable, permanently connected battery alarm. A new owner has six months from the date the title transfers to fit the right number of alarms.

Hard-wired alarms must be installed by a qualified electrician. The MFS recommends replacing all domestic smoke alarms after 10 years, whatever their power source. The [SA Government](https://www.sa.gov.au/topics/housing/renting-and-letting/renting-privately/during-a-tenancy/Repairs-and-maintenance) also suggests checking that smoke alarms work at each routine inspection.

## Locks and security

Under section 66 of the [Residential Tenancies Act](https://www.legislation.sa.gov.au/_legislation-documents/lz/c/a/residential-tenancies-act-1995/current/1995.63.auth.pdf), every tenancy includes a term that the landlord will take reasonable steps to provide and maintain the locks and other devices needed to keep the property reasonably secure.

Neither landlord nor tenant can change the locks without the other's consent, and neither can unreasonably refuse consent. Since 1 July 2024 there's an exception for a person protected by an intervention order, who can change the locks without consent (section 66A).

Before letting, make sure every lock works and you have a working key for each one.

## Repairs once the tenant is in

The tenant should report repairs to you or your agent. For non-urgent repairs, CBS has a [request for repairs](https://www.cbs.sa.gov.au/documents/tenancy/forms/Request_for_repairs.pdf) form. Urgent repairs, such as a gas leak, should be reported straight away.

If the landlord doesn't deal with an urgent repair, or can't be contacted, the tenant may have it done by a licensed tradesperson. A tenant can also apply to SACAT to have repairs carried out, for compensation, or to end the tenancy. The CBS [Tenant Information Guide](https://www.cbs.sa.gov.au/documents/tenancy/guides/Tenant-Information-Guide.pdf) explains the process.

Wear and tear is the landlord's to fix. Damage a tenant causes deliberately or through carelessness is theirs to fix.
