Yes, a landlord in South Australia can refuse a tenant's request to keep a pet, but only on specific grounds set out in the law, and only in writing within 14 days. Since 1 July 2024, "no pets" on its own isn't a valid reason, you can't charge a separate pet bond, and a request you don't answer properly in time is treated as approved.

Here's how the rules work for landlords as at October 2026.

## How a tenant asks to keep a pet

Under the [Residential Tenancies Act 1995](https://www.legislation.sa.gov.au/__legislation/lz/c/a/residential%20tenancies%20act%201995/current/1995.63.auth.pdf), a tenant can keep a pet with the landlord's approval. They can ask when they apply for the property or at any time during the tenancy, by giving you a completed [Application for approval to keep a pet on rental premises](https://www.cbs.sa.gov.au/documents/tenancy/forms/Application-for-approval-to-keep-a-pet-on-rental-premises.pdf) ([Law Handbook: Pets](https://lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s06s06.php)).

Two things to know before a request even arrives:

- **Assistance and therapeutic animals don't need approval.** A tenant can keep an assistance animal or therapeutic animal (as defined in the Equal Opportunity Act 1984) with or without your approval. The pet rules below don't apply to them.
- **You can't advertise "no pets allowed".** Consumer and Business Services (CBS) says a landlord or agent can't state "no pets allowed" unless pets are specifically prohibited by the property's strata, community title, rooming house or residential park rules ([CBS: Renting with pets](https://cbs.sa.gov.au/sections/renting/renting/renting-with-pets)).

## You have 14 days to respond

Within 14 days of receiving the request, you (or your property manager) must do one of three things:

1. Approve it
2. Approve it with reasonable conditions
3. Refuse it on one of the prescribed grounds

If you refuse, you must set out in writing which grounds apply and why you believe they apply. CBS has a [Notice of response to pet application](https://www.cbs.sa.gov.au/documents/tenancy/forms/Notice-of-response-to-pet-application.pdf) form for this.

According to [CBS](https://cbs.sa.gov.au/sections/renting/renting/renting-with-pets), if you don't respond in writing within 14 days, or your response doesn't meet the legal requirements (approved reasons for a refusal, or reasons for any conditions), the tenant's request is taken as approved.

## When a landlord can refuse a pet

You can only refuse on these grounds ([Law Handbook: Pets](https://lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s06s06.php), section 66D of the Act):

- Keeping the pet would exceed a reasonable number of animals at the premises
- The premises aren't suitable because there isn't appropriate fencing, open space or something else needed to keep the pet humanely
- The pet would pose an unacceptable risk to someone's health and safety (for example, because it's venomous)
- Keeping the pet would break a law, by-law, council rule or other restriction that applies to the premises
- The tenant hasn't agreed to reasonable conditions you've proposed
- The animal in the request isn't a pet
- The premises is a moveable dwelling and the pet would break a licence condition

"I'd rather not have pets" isn't on the list. [CBS guidance](https://cbs.sa.gov.au/sections/renting/renting/renting-with-pets) is clear that stating "no pets are allowed", or that a pet isn't your preference, isn't a ground to refuse.

If a tenant thinks a refusal or a condition is unreasonable, they can apply to the [South Australian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (SACAT)](https://www.sacat.sa.gov.au/) for a review.

## Conditions you can set

Conditions must be reasonable, set out in writing, and based on the type of pet and the nature of the property. The Act gives examples of conditions that are reasonable:

- The pet must be restrained when you or your agent enter the property
- The pet must be kept outside, unless it's the kind of animal that's ordinarily kept inside
- If the pet is allowed inside, the carpets must be professionally cleaned at the end of the tenancy

The [Law Handbook](https://lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01.php) explains that a general lease clause requiring professional carpet cleaning at the end of every tenancy is unenforceable, because the Act only requires the property to be left reasonably clean. A pet condition is the proper way to ask for it.

### What you can't ask for

[CBS](https://cbs.sa.gov.au/sections/renting/renting/renting-with-pets) lists conditions a landlord can't impose. You can't ask the tenant to:

- Pay higher rent or a higher bond, or any additional fee or charge
- Pay a separate pet bond
- Pay penalties or a pre-agreed amount for damage
- Buy goods or services from you or a specific person or business
- Pay you an incentive
- Provide any other form of security

For what you can charge as bond in the first place, see our guide to [rental bonds for SA landlords](/blog/rental-bonds-sa-landlords/).

### Once approved, approval lasts

Under section 66F of the Act, once a pet is approved, the approval continues for the life of the pet. [CHECK: confirm against s 66F which events (e.g. a new tenancy agreement, a change of landlord or agent) are listed as not affecting the approval.]

Section 66G also says a landlord or agent has no additional duty of care to a person arising from a pet approval or a SACAT order about a pet.

## What if a tenant keeps a pet without approval?

Under the [Residential Tenancies Regulations](https://lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01.php), a tenant keeping a pet on the premises without authorisation is one of the prescribed reasons a landlord can use to:

- End a **periodic** tenancy, with at least 90 days' notice (Form 8)
- End a **fixed-term** tenancy at the end of the term, with at least 60 days' notice (Form 9)

In practice, the first step is usually a conversation. Inviting the tenant to lodge an application lets you assess the pet against the grounds and conditions above.

A tenant can also ask SACAT to declare a termination notice has no effect if it's retaliatory ([Law Handbook](https://lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01.php)), so get advice before ending a tenancy over a pet.

## Pet damage at the end of a tenancy

There's no separate pet bond, so the general rules on damage and the bond are what apply. Under the Act, a tenant must not intentionally or negligently cause damage, must tell you about damage, and must return the property reasonably clean. They're responsible for damage caused intentionally or negligently, but not for fair wear and tear or a genuine accident ([Law Handbook](https://lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01.php)).

The bond is security against damage by the tenant, unpaid rent, the property not being left reasonably clean and other losses that are the tenant's responsibility. At the end of the tenancy, the bond is claimed through CBS's Residential Bonds Online. If the other party disputes the claim, CBS tries to conciliate, and the matter can be referred to SACAT.

That's why a thorough entry inspection sheet matters: it's the record you compare against at the end.
