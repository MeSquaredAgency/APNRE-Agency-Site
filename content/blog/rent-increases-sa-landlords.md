In South Australia, a landlord can increase the rent once every 12 months at most, and must give the tenant at least 60 days' written notice of the new amount and the date it starts. There's no cap on how much rent can go up, but a tenant can ask the South Australian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (SACAT) to rule that an increase is excessive. These rules come from the [Residential Tenancies Act 1995](https://www.legislation.sa.gov.au/lz?path=%2Fc%2Fa%2Fresidential%20tenancies%20act%201995) and are summarised by the SA Government on its [rent increases page](https://www.sa.gov.au/topics/housing/renting-and-letting/renting-privately/during-a-tenancy/rent-increases).

Here's how it works in practice for landlords, as at October 2026.

## How often can rent be increased in SA?

Rent can't be increased in the first 12 months of a tenancy, and each increase after that has to be at least 12 months after the last one. That applies whether the lease is fixed-term or periodic ([Law Handbook: Rent](https://www.lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s06s05.php), citing section 55 of the Act).

Signing a new lease doesn't restart the clock. A series of agreements between the same landlord and tenant is treated as one agreement unless at least 12 months have passed since the rent was last set or increased ([Law Handbook: Rent](https://www.lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s06s05.php)).

The same 12-month limit applies when you and the tenant agree to an increase. A landlord and tenant can agree to raise the rent at any time, but not within the first 12 months of the tenancy or within 12 months of the last increase ([Law Handbook: Rent](https://www.lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s06s05.php)).

Minor changes to the tenancy laws started on 15 January 2026. Consumer and Business Services (CBS) says these make clear that the once-a-year limit also applies when a fixed-term lease becomes periodic, and that you can't agree in advance to a series of increases within a year ([CBS rental reforms](https://www.cbs.sa.gov.au/campaigns/rental-reforms)). ## How much notice do you need to give, and in what form?

The tenant must get at least **60 days' written notice**. The notice must state the amount of the increase and the day it takes effect ([Law Handbook: Rent](https://www.lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s06s05.php); [sa.gov.au](https://www.sa.gov.au/topics/housing/renting-and-letting/renting-privately/during-a-tenancy/rent-increases)).

CBS publishes a [Notice to tenant of rent increase](https://www.cbs.sa.gov.au/documents/tenancy/forms/Notice_of_rent_increase.pdf) form under section 55 of the Act. It sets out the new rent, how often it's paid and when it starts, and lists the ways the notice can be given. The two time limits work together. The new rent can't start until at least 60 days after the notice is given, and at least 12 months after the tenancy began or the rent last went up. Allow for both when you plan a review, so the notice goes out in time.

## Fixed-term vs periodic leases

**Fixed-term lease.** You can only increase the rent during a fixed term if the agreement specifically allows for it ([Law Handbook: Rent](https://www.lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s06s05.php), section 55(2)(b)). CBS also says the clause must show how any increase will be calculated ([CBS summary of tenancy reforms for landlords and agents](https://cbs.sa.gov.au/documents/tenancy/fact-sheets/Summary-of-tenancy-reforms-landlords-and-agents.pdf)). [CHECK: confirm the "how it will be calculated" requirement against the full CBS fact sheet.] If the lease says nothing about rent increases, the rent stays the same until the fixed term ends.

**Periodic lease.** If a fixed term isn't ended and isn't renewed, the agreement carries on as a periodic tenancy ([Law Handbook: Fixed term or periodic tenancies](https://www.lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s02.php)). The 12-month and 60-day rules still apply to a periodic tenancy.

If you want room to review the rent during a longer lease, deal with it when the lease is drawn up, not partway through. For the wider changes to SA renting laws, see our guide to the [SA rental reforms for landlords](/blog/sa-rental-reforms-for-landlords/).

## Rent bidding is banned

Rent bidding rules apply when you advertise a property, not during a tenancy, but they affect how you set the starting rent.

A landlord or agent must advertise or offer a rental at a **fixed amount**, and must not ask for or invite an offer of more rent than the advertised amount. The maximum penalty is a $20,000 fine. Third parties such as rental application platforms also can't rate a prospective tenant based on their willingness to pay more than the fixed rent ([Law Handbook: Offering premises for rent](https://www.lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s04s01.php), sections 52A and 52B).

So the advertised rent needs to be right the first time. You can't advertise low and let applicants bid it up.

## What can a tenant do if they think the increase is excessive?

A tenant can apply to [SACAT](https://www.sacat.sa.gov.au/case-type/housing-and-rentals/excessive-rent-applications) for an order that the rent is excessive. If the application is about an increase, it must be made **within 90 days** of the notice being given ([Law Handbook: Rent](https://www.lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s06s05.php), section 56).

According to the [Law Handbook](https://www.lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s06s05.php) and [SACAT](https://www.sacat.sa.gov.au/case-type/housing-and-rentals/excessive-rent-applications), the factors SACAT considers include:

- rents for comparable properties in the same or similar areas
- the value of the property
- its condition and state of repair
- whether the tenant was pressured into agreeing to the increase
- whether the increase is disproportionate to the rent being paid

If SACAT finds the rent excessive, it can set a maximum rent for up to one year ([Law Handbook: Rent](https://www.lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch23s01s06s05.php)).

## How to set a fair rent

The best protection against a dispute is a rent you can back up. SACAT's first factor is what comparable properties nearby rent for, so check the market:

- **Look at recent comparable leases**, not just asking prices, for similar homes in the same or nearby suburbs.
- **Be honest about condition.** A property that's been kept up supports a higher rent than one with outstanding repairs.
- **Weigh the cost of a vacancy.** A few dollars more a week isn't worth it if a good tenant leaves and the property sits empty.
- **Keep a record** of how you reached the figure, in case you're ever asked.

## Getting the paperwork right

Most rent increase problems come down to timing: notice given too late, an increase within 12 months, or a fixed-term lease with no increase clause. Plan reviews around the lease dates and keep a copy of every notice.
